Curved Triangles

  1. Edges. Every quad edge is one ellipse arc, shaped by the normals at its two ends. The arc lies in the plane through the edge and the average of the two end normals, and leaves each end at a right angle to the normal there. Where one arc can't do it, the edge becomes two arcs joined at a middle point. On a sharp edge (two different normals at an end) the arc leaves along the one direction both faces allow.
  2. Inside a quad. A centre point is placed where the arc from the bottom edge's middle to the top's and the arc from the right edge's middle to the left's pass each other. An arc runs from every corner and every edge middle to the centre. That cuts the quad into curved triangles, each bounded by three arcs.
  3. A triangle's surface is defined by its three arcs alone. Nothing is fitted, so neighbours always share their edge exactly.
  4. Pixel corners. The vertical and the horizontal grid line through a pixel corner each cut a triangle along a simple curve. Where the two curves meet is the surface point: one cubic, one square root, two quadratics. No iteration.
  5. u, v is the quad's own, 0…1 across the whole quad, fixed to the surface. The checker shows it: orbit, and it must stay put on the shape.

Each quad has its own tint; the checker is 8 × 8 squares of the quad's u, v. A pixel is drawn from its 4 corners; a pixel with only some corners on the surface is drawn fainter.

Not in this page: rounding of sharp edges (the blending distance), handle length as bulge, and exact coverage of partly covered pixels (here: the share of corners that hit). Each triangle solves every corner inside the screen box of its 6 control points; narrowing that to its exact spans is not built. A triangle with a control point behind the camera is skipped and counted.
