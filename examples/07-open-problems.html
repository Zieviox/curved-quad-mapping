Open Problems Explained

Pictures 1 and 2 are computed live from the real pieces (the step 10 conversion, unchanged). Picture 3 is a simplified 2D cut that shows the mechanism.

1. Cracks: why some pieces can't close

Magnify gap
Shared arc (both pieces agree on it)Where piece A's surface really endsWhere piece B's surface really endsThe crack between them

  1. Two neighbouring pieces share one border arc, computed once from the same two handles. On paper they meet exactly.
  2. Each piece is one ellipsoid, and it must contain all 4 of its border arcs.
  3. At each corner of a piece, two of its arcs meet at one point. One ellipsoid can hold two such arcs only if they meet twice (the two-point rule). Usually they don't.
  4. So the ellipsoid follows some arcs and misses others. Its real edge (orange) leaves the shared arc (blue).
  5. The neighbour does the same, but misses differently (purple). Two different misses on one border open a gap: the crack (red).
On the cube, the rounded box and the wave every piece's arcs do meet the rule (spheres, cylinders, planes), so the orange and purple lines lie on the blue one and there's no crack. On the mixed cube the handles (diagonal below, horizontal above) give arcs that can't share one ellipsoid.

2. Length × 2: pieces with no surface inside

The piece's fitted shape, cut through its middleThe piece as its arcs describe itLines that pick (u, v) = 0, 0.25 … 1HitMiss = hole

  1. Normal length × 2 makes every arc bulge harder. Its weight w goes above 1, so each arc becomes a piece of a hyperbola instead of an ellipse.
  2. The fit then has to find one quadric through 4 hyperbola arcs. The best it finds here is a shape whose cut (black) has two separate branches, with empty space between them.
  3. The piece's edges sit on those branches, but its middle (blue) runs through the empty space.
  4. A point (u, v) is where a line meets the shape. Lines through the middle find nothing there (red X): the piece has no surface inside, which is the hole count in step 10.
This isn't a drawing or rounding problem: the fitted shape really has no points there. Splitting doesn't help either, because the arcs stay hyperbolas. It's the same compatibility problem as cracks, in a stronger form.

3. The far side: why a hit can be on the wrong side

The pieceBetween its side planesCorrect hitFar-side hit that also passes the plane test
  1. A pixel's ray hits a piece's ellipsoid at two points: where it enters and where it leaves.
  2. The piece keeps a hit if it lies between its 4 side planes (shaded). That's the cheap test that also gives (u, v).
  3. But the side planes don't meet at the ellipsoid's centre. Here they meet above the piece, so the shaded region widens with depth and reaches the far side too. The far hit (red) passes the plane test as well.
  4. Today each accepted hit is checked by running the piece's forward map at its (u, v) and keeping it only if that lands on the hit. That's correct, but it costs one extra line-vs-quadric per hit.
Cheaper rule to test next: the forward map always picks the same one of a line's two hits. At that hit, the shape's slope along the line has a fixed sign for the whole piece. So a sign check of the gradient (∇Q · line direction, about 6 FMA) might replace the extra forward map. Needs checking on all meshes before trusting it.
